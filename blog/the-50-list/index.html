There Are 50 Things Your Kids Need to Do With You. Most Dads Miss Half of Them.

There are 50 experiences every father should have with his kids before they leave home. Most dads know about a handful. Here's the full list, with the developmental window for each one.

Most dads don't have a list.

They have intentions. They have memories of what their own father did or didn't do. They have vague ideas that some Saturday they'll go fishing, or teach their son to change a tire, or take their daughter camping before she's too old to think it's cool.

But good intentions without a list aren't a plan. They're a wish.

The difference between a dad who does those things and a dad who doesn't usually isn't love, effort, or even time. It's specificity. The dads who actually show up for the right moments know what the moments are. They've thought through what matters. They've made it concrete.

That's what the 50-List is.

What the 50-List Is (and What It Isn't)

The 50-List is not a generic bucket list. It's not "go to Disney World" or "build a birdhouse." Those are fine things. They're just not what this is.

The 50-List is a set of experiences (specific, intentional, developmentally grounded) that show up in the research on what kids actually remember about their fathers. Not the things they remember doing. The things that stay with them. The ones that shape who they become.

Some of these are milestone experiences you only get one shot at. Some are recurring habits that compound over years. A few will feel obvious once you read them; you'll realize you've been meaning to do them for two years and haven't.

The key factor most dad-content misses: these experiences have windows. A 12-year-old boy will camp with you. A 15-year-old probably won't, not for the same reason, anyway. Some of the most important things you can do with your kids stop landing right somewhere between 9 and 11, when the world starts competing louder.

This list is organized by when each experience matters most, not just whether it matters.

The 50-List: Preview (10 of 50)

The complete 50-List is built into ANCHR, organized by your kid's age. Here's a preview of what's on it, and why each one is on there.

1. The First Hard Conversation You Don't Dodge (Ages 6–10)

At some point your kid is going to ask about death, or divorce, or why people hurt each other, or whether God is real. Most dads deflect. They give the safe answer, the short answer, or the "ask your mom" answer.

The dads who earn trust sit down, take a breath, and say: "That's a really important question. Here's what I actually think."

You don't have to have the right answer. You have to model what it looks like to take hard questions seriously. That's the deposit.

2. Teaching Them a Skill You're Actually Good At (Ages 5–12)

Not a skill you think they should have. A skill you have. Something real, something you do well, something that takes time to learn.

It could be fishing, woodworking, cooking a specific dish, throwing a spiral, playing a chord, fixing a leaky faucet. The content doesn't matter as much as the dynamic: you in teacher mode, them watching and trying and failing and learning.

This is where kids see their father as competent, not just authoritative. That matters more than most parents realize.

3. A Trip That's Harder Than It Needs to Be (Ages 7–13)

Not a resort. Something with friction. A long hike, a camping trip where you forget something important, a road trip to a place that turns out to be less impressive than the map suggested.

Hard trips produce stories. Stories produce identity. "My dad and I once drove 6 hours and the place was closed, so we just drove around until we found something else" is a better memory than most planned vacations.

The difficulty is the point.

4. Letting Them See You Apologize (Any Age, But Start Young)

This one costs you something in the moment. It pays forward for decades.

When you're wrong (when you snap, when you overreact, when you make a call you shouldn't have), naming it directly with your kid is one of the most powerful things you can do. Not vaguely. Not "if I hurt your feelings." Specifically.

"I yelled at you this morning and that wasn't fair. I was stressed about something else and I took it out on you. I'm sorry."

That's the model. They'll use it their whole lives.

5. One Thing They're Better at Than You (Ages 8–14)

At some point your kid will surpass you at something. It might be a video game, a sport, a subject at school, a creative skill.

The dads who handle this well say: "You're better at this than I am. Show me how you do it."

They hand control over. They learn from their kid. They signal that competence earns respect in this family, not just age.

That moment of reversal, done right, is a landmark in your relationship.

6. A Night They Get to Be in Charge (Ages 8–12)

One night, fully handed over. They pick dinner. They pick the activity. They decide the schedule.

No suggestions. No redirects. No steering toward what you think would be better.

What you're teaching: that their preferences are worth taking seriously. What you're learning: who your kid actually is when no one's guiding the agenda.

This one surprises most dads.

7. The Talk About Your Own Father (Ages 10–15)

Your kid should know where you came from. Not the sanitized version: the real version. What your father was like. What he did well. What he got wrong. What you decided to carry forward and what you decided to leave behind.

This isn't about winning sympathy or explaining your flaws. It's about giving them context. It shows them that families are works in progress, that character is chosen, and that the patterns can change.

Most dads never have this conversation. The ones who do create a kind of trust that doesn't exist any other way.

8. A Regular One-on-One Rhythm, Locked In (Ages 5 Through Departure)

Weekly, biweekly, whatever fits. Same thing, same time, their choice of activity. Exclusive to them, not the group, not the siblings.

The regularity is what signals: you're not a special occasion. You're a constant.

Research on father-child attachment consistently shows that predictable, recurring one-on-one time matters more than the amount of total family time. Exclusivity is the point.

9. Doing Something You're Bad at Together (Ages 6–12)

Pick something neither of you can do. Take a class, try a new sport, learn a skill that makes both of you look like beginners.

This does two things. First, it lets your kid see you be incompetent and unbothered: one of the most useful things a father can model. Second, it puts you on the same footing. You're both learning. The authority gradient disappears.

Some of the most honest conversations happen in spaces where neither person is the expert.

10. Telling Them Specifically What You See in Them (Ages 8–16)

Not "I'm proud of you." Not "good job." Specific.

"I noticed how you handled that situation with your friend. You didn't overreact. You stayed calm. That's a kind of strength most adults don't have."

Specific observation → named quality → why it matters. That's the template.

Generic praise is noise. Specific acknowledgment of a quality you actually see in your child is something they'll remember word for word at 35.

Why Most Dads Miss These

These aren't hard. Most of them require no money, no equipment, no special skill. They require presence, intention, and a small amount of planning.

The reason they get missed isn't indifference. It's default. Most dads are running the default program (work, provide, protect, survive the week), and the default program doesn't include any of this. It's not designed to. It was designed for a different era.

What you need is a system that prompts you, tracks what you've done, and reminds you what's coming up while there's still time.
